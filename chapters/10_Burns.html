Burns

The copper wash-vats in the basement beneath the Sham Shui Po apothecary simmered over low gas rings, filling the narrow stone cellar with the pungent, stinging steam of boiled white vinegar and crushed camphor leaves. Above the low rafters, the rhythmic clatter of the morning industrial laundry sent fine plaster dust drifting through the damp air, settling across rolls of unbleached cotton gauze and rusted zinc tubs. Velvet sat on an overturned tea crate in the corner, elbows resting on his knees, breathing in the sour heat to scour the taste of scorched insulation from the back of his throat.

Stripping away two distinct lives in less than six hours had extracted an unforgiving biological tax.

The elder dignity of Zhang Wei had died first, shattered in the cordite haze and splintered shelving of the Jade Crane storage facility when the Red Dragon enforcers drew down. Then, on the rain-slicked concrete of Kwai Chung lot CT4, Leung Hoi-ching’s working-class harbor existence had been incinerated under the blinding, concussive fury of a summoned gale. When the air spirit’s lightning arced across the container stacks, strobing the berths in violet-white, fleeing syndicate soldiers had caught Hoi-ching’s face under the security floods just as corporate security patrol sirens began their wail.

Velvet reached up with bare, trembling fingers, unfastening the stained high-visibility vest and grease-marked linen shirt of the harbor clerk. He dropped them into the iron drain at his feet and released the active spell-work.

The cosmetic matrix dissolved from his facial tissue with a dry, searing heat. Deep within the zygomatics, the compressed bone-latches released, clicking heavily through the skull as his facial geometry widened. The broadened mandibular frame and shortened clavicles of Hoi-ching eased back into their natural alignment, spinal vertebrae settling with a dull, grinding throb that radiated down into his lower back. He closed his eyes against the sharp migraine behind his temples, waiting as the dark obsidian pigment flooded back into the outer rings of his irises, washing away the false amber of the docks until only the raw, un-sculpted baseline of Kim Jin-Young remained.

On the rough pine table beside the wash-vat, three certified credsticks lay beside an empty brass burner.

The ledger was an operational disaster. Mr. Fa’s panicked transfer had cleared at seven thousand nuyen, but the shadows in Hong Kong demanded their toll in blood and silver. One thousand had vanished into the coat pocket of a Port Authority watch commander at the outer gate; crossroad silver pressed into a nervous palm to ensure the security subroutines logging their vehicle were purged before CorpSec arrived. Another two thousand had gone directly to the elderly herbalist upstairs for clean saline, sulfur burn pastes, sterile shears, and dry woolen blankets. Factoring in transit chits and ferry bribes to move their human cargo south of the cordon, the contract had closed at a net financial loss.

Mitsuhama’s actuarial routines, hardwired into his early education, categorized the deficit as an irrecoverable failure of resource management. Yet looking at the red balances on his commlink slate, his pulse was a slow, unwavering core. The nuyen was simply spent tallow, burned to keep the wolves off the threshold.

Across the stone floor, huddled against the warmth of the hot-water pipes, sat seven street kids and runaways pulled from the belly of shipping container CT4.

They were silent, wrapped in coarse grey wool, their small frames shivering with the lingering static aftershocks of the dockland assault. The storm had breached the perimeter, but an air spirit possessed no concept of thermal conductivity. When the lightning had struck the corrugated alloy of the holding pens, the current had surged through the structural ribs, superheating the interior metal and showering the captives in white-hot molten slag.

A fifteen-year-old girl near the boiler hissed in pain, clutching a forearm where charred cotton had fused directly into blistered, weeping skin.

Velvet stood up from the crate. His legs were stiff from the cartilage reset, but the tremors in his hands were a lingering reflex of corporate adrenaline that needed to be cleared. In Mitsuhama’s idol facilities, coaxing the spirits had been drilled as a cold, biomechanical protocol: align the mental vectors, force the mana through neural pathways like high-voltage current, and bind the external entity as a disposable battery to bear the feedback.

He stepped to the center of the flagstones, lowered his chin, and emptied the vessel. He cleared the floor of his own ego, relaxing the tension in his diaphragm and inviting the spirits across the threshold.

The air in the cellar shifted. The caustic tang of vinegar and zinc softened, replaced by an ancient warmth carrying the dry scent of wild cedar drying over an autumn hearth. For once there was no icy, burning back-voltage of corporate spellcraft; it settled across his collarbones with the comforting weight of a heavy wool blanket. His posture shifted reflexively, his breathing deepening as an unhurried, ancestral stillness anchored his frame.

The spiritual touch moved across his skin in a soft, unhurried invitation. Velvet released his hold on his cosmetic spell-work, allowing the spirit to take hold. Beneath the skin, the metahuman geometry yielded. The tapered cartilage of his ears softened and drew inward, rounding into plain human lobes, while the obsidian depth of his Daleketnon irises washed into a warm, everyday tea-brown. Her ribcage pulled inward, bringing the shoulders into a gentle curve, settling into the frame of an ordinary human woman. Her skin took on the dry, warm comfort of unglazed earthenware, radiating a subtle stillness that pushed back the cellar’s chill. The calculating lines of his face yielded, the jawline tapering and cheekbones rounding into the quiet, unadorned composure of an herbalist.

Velvet knelt on the cold stone before the injured girl. When her hands reached out, the tremors were gone. Her fingers moved with an ancient, rhythmic economy she had never studied in corporate training. With a pair of dull brass shears, she cut away the blackened sleeve, her touch so light and steady that the girl’s sharp gasps slowed to quiet whimpers. She dipped a clean linen cloth into a basin of cold herbal tea and boiled saline, cleansing the soot and metal flakes from the raw tissue.

The spirit investing her turned her unearthly presence inward, dampening the active glare of her aura until it became a deep, sedative pool. The room’s ambient anxiety began to unwind. Across the flagstones, the other children stopped their restless shifting, their rapid heart rates slowing in response to the quiet, immovable peace radiating from the kneeling figure.

With unhurried precision, Velvet applied thick coats of cooling camphor salve across the burns, smoothing strips of unbleached cotton over the wounds. When she tied the finishing knots at the wrist, her fingers moved in a distinct, looping braid. It was an old method of untying tension and binding trauma that owed nothing to modern plaster casts.

She moved to the next child, an underfed boy nursing a dislocated shoulder suffered when the container doors were blown inward. Velvet placed one warm palm flat against the boy’s sternum, letting the ancestral weight transfer through the touch to ground the child’s panic. With a single, fluid traction of her other hand against the wrist, the joint slipped back into its socket with a soft, clean pop. The boy flinched, blinked through tears, and then let his head fall forward against Velvet’s shoulder, exhausted beyond fear.

In the corner of the ceiling, a grease-stained trid monitor flickered to life, its audio feed turned low against the hum of the boiler.

The Hong Kong news feed displayed sweeping aerial footage of the Kwai Chung container terminal. Emergency strobes from dozens of Port Authority cruisers painted the gantry cranes in flashing blue, while heavy corporate drones hovered over the charred, twisted wreckage of lot CT4. A crisp, polished anchor praised the swift intervention of local authorities in breaking up a syndicate human trafficking ring, cutting briefly to an interview excerpt authored by Fa Ren, the journalism student whose university thesis had broken the story wide open. On screen, Fa Ren stood outside her father’s apartment, exhausted but resolute, speaking of accountability and corporate negligence with fierce, articulate clarity.

Velvet kept her hands steady on the gauze. She tied the final bandage, smoothed the wool blanket over the boy’s shoulders, and stepped back. The ancestral presence receded like a slow, warming tide, leaving her muscles sore but her center clear. All seven children were asleep, their breathing rhythmic and deep against the low hiss of the copper vats.

She walked to the narrow wooden stairs leading up to the street. She pulled on a plain, dark windbreaker borrowed from the apothecary’s drying rack and zipped it to her throat. She had no active face in this sprawl, no remaining corporate identification, and barely enough nuyen to buy passage on the morning ferry.

She paused at the final landing where a full-length mirror lay against the wall. Her eyes took in the form of the delicate, soft featured young woman looking back at her, her short, dark hair plastered to her forehead by sweat and steam from the boiling vats. A small shadow creased her brow as she stared at the reflection, the corners of her mouth turning downward in a barely perceptible frown. With a quiet sigh, she reached up and gently touched her cheek, her expression unreadable as she took in the changes wrought by the spirits, before turning away and ascending the stairs to the street.

“What is your name?” Velvet mused at her reflection. Unexpectedly, her mouth moved on its own accord. Her lips formed the shape of syllables: Kang Anning.

Pushing open the heavy alley door, Velvet stepped out into the damp, sulfur-tinted dawn of Kowloon. Across Victoria Harbour, the emerald spire of the Wuxing Skytower caught the first pale light of morning, cold and untouchable above the mist. Velvet pulled her hood low against the harbor drizzle, turned her back on the tower, and walked down into the crowded streets with clean hands, an empty ledger, and an unburdened soul.

# First Negotiation

Tanaka Ryo kept the unbadged Suzuki idling between his knees in the Redmond alley, the engine's rhythmic vibration rattling loose fairing bolts against the cold steel frame. Across the polarized visor of his Shoei helmet, pirated AR spam flickered through the sulfur drizzle, with glitching advertisements for Stuffer Shack ramen and second-hand cyberware fighting for bandwidth in the local mesh. The exhaust pipe gave off a faint metallic ping as it cooled against the wet air. He cut the ignition with a turn of the key but left the helmet locked down tight over his chin; in the Barrens, bare features were an invitation to street violence, while features sculpted with corporate symmetry were a magnetic disturbance that drew eyes from across a crowded room.

Inside the *Red Line*, sub-floor bass vibrated through the grease-stained floorboards from a jukebox looping thirty-year-old Neo-Tokyo synth-pop. Still wearing the dark, faceless dome of his riding helmet, he paused near a dead arcade terminal in the corner, shoulders slouched to mimic a delivery courier waiting on a delayed package. In the back booth beneath a flickering Shiawase promotional banner sat the Johnson, wearing a surplus wool overcoat with the frayed stitch scars of a NeoNet security patch cut from the lapel. The man nursed a glass of amber synthetic rye, his eyes darting between the front door and the bar taps with the ingrained nervousness of an ex-corporate middle manager stranded outside the grid.

Through the smoked shield of his visor, Ryo watched him in the scratched reflection of the arcade glass. When a heavy-set ork mercenary in armored ballistic plates pushed past the aisle, the Johnson’s aura flared with jagged, defensive orange, his right hand twitching toward an illegal pocket holster beneath his coat. But when the bar's floor manager stepped over to clear an empty beer bottle, the man’s posture shifted instinctively—his spine straightened, chin dipping into the reflexive, ingrained deference of a salaryman accustomed to taking directives from executive suites. A heavy masculine runner would trigger his panic and draw a defensive standoff, whereas a corporate woman with clean, icy composure would slide beneath his guard and command the table.

Ryo slipped past the bar toward the narrow corridor leading to the rear storage rooms. The restroom was a damp concrete cell lit by a single buzzing fluorescent tube that threw grey shadows across stained tile, where cold water dripped steadily from an overhead copper pipe into a cracked ceramic urinal. He locked the iron latch, unclipped the Shoei helmet, and set it on the water tank before stripping off his heavy riding leathers. He initiated the shift as his Transys Avalon commlink re-keyed the local PAN registers to broadcast the clean, forged SIN of Lee Ji-yoo. The physical toll struck his marrow with dry, burning friction as the cosmetic spell engaged: Ryo gripped the cold porcelain edges of the sink, knuckles whitening as structural magic compressed his ribcage with a grinding ache, vertebra settling against vertebra while cartilage in his larynx aligned with a heavy click that thinned his breath to a lighter register.

She opened her eyes in the stained mirror, waiting three slow breaths for the momentary vertigo to clear while the bone-latch behind her ears cooled. She smoothed the dark silk shell over her narrowed shoulders, left the heavy riding jacket folded behind the exposed water pipes, and stepped into flat leather shoes. Unlocking the iron door, she walked back into the smoky gloom of the bar with the quiet, acoustic poise of an executive assistant entering a boardroom, slipping into the booth opposite the Johnson with silent, fluid grace. The man looked up, his hand freezing on the rim of his glass as the red neon from the bar sign caught her face—clean, balanced, without a single blemish, scar, or line of street fatigue. His throat moved as he swallowed, fingers slipping off the synthetic rye as his nervous system struggled to recalibrate under the sudden, focused weight of her presence.

"You're late," he said, but the words carried no weight against the stillness across the table.

"Knight Errant sweeps were active along the perimeter highway," Ji-yoo said, her voice clear, level, and entirely devoid of street slang. "The transit took longer to avoid automated checkpoints."

The Johnson cleared his throat, wiped a sheen of cold sweat from his palm against his wool trousers, and slid a battered Sony datapad across the cedar laminate. "Renraku storage node in Bellevue. Ten thousand nuyen. Five thousand up front for operational expenses, five on delivery."

Ji-yoo glanced at the encrypted node coordinates on the screen, then rested her hands flat and open on the table. "Eight thousand," she said.

The Johnson blinked, his brow furrowing as he leaned back. "What?"

"Eight thousand," she repeated, her tone calm and conversational. "And no advance."

The Johnson pulled back into the corner of the booth, his eyes narrowing with sharp corporate suspicion. Street runners fought over every scrip, demanding up-front deposits for ammo, forged credentials, and decker fees. A runner who negotiated the price downward was an operative with an unstated agenda.

"Runners don't negotiate down," the Johnson said, his grip tightening on the table edge. "Who are you working for?"

"Clean retrieval," Ji-yoo said, holding his gaze with unblinking composure. "Renraku will not log the intrusion as an external breach. When the datapad is delivered, you owe me a marker. One favor, on demand, within your departmental authority."

The Johnson sat motionless. The immediate relief of saving corporate operating budget died, replaced by the heavy understanding of personal debt. Nuyen was spent and forgotten; a personal marker was much longer lasting. He looked at the quiet poise across the table, the complete absence of street desperation, and gave a single, tight nod.

"One marker," he said, sliding the pad fully across the wood. "We have an agreement."

Ji-yoo picked up the datapad, slipped it into her coat pocket, and stood. She walked out into the Redmond drizzle, leaving the Johnson alone with his synthetic rye beneath the glitching neon.

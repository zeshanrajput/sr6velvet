09. Tea in Tacoma

The salt fog coming off Commencement Bay smelled of creosote, wet iron, and the heavy diesel exhaust of container ships idling at the Tacoma docks. It rolled up the dark slope of the waterfront, wrapping around the low wooden roof of Xingfu Chaguan and muffling the sound of the automated gantry cranes two miles out in the harbor.

Inside the private backroom, the ambient noise of the sprawl died completely. The room was lined with thick, old-growth cedar panels, completely devoid of Matrix displays or visible terminal interfaces. A small cast-iron kettle sat over a silent charcoal burner, sending a thin, steady thread of steam toward the bamboo ceiling.

Tanaka Ryo sat on a low floor cushion across from Ni Ni Xiaolu.

He sat in his masculine baseline profile, configured by Mitsuhama’s branding team to project cross-cultural authority. His jawline was planed to a mathematical symmetry, his dark hair brushed back, his shoulders broad beneath an unbadged silk coat. Beneath the active discipline of his posture, the quiet ache of Cosmetic Control hummed behind his ears, a dull metallic heat where bone and cartilage met the spell-work.

Across the low cedar table, Ni Ni Xiaolu sat perfectly straight in a crisp, zebra-patterned blazer over a white silk blouse. Her dark hair was pinned back with ivory needles, her eyes reflecting the dim amber glow of the charcoal burner. She moved with the fluid, predatory stillness of a 426 Red Pole lieutenant who had spent a decade surviving the internal meat-grinder of the Octagon Triad.

Resting on the dark wood between them, six inches from her right hand, lay her customized Ares Predator heavy pistol. The mother-of-pearl handle inlays caught the firelight, glowing like damp bone against the cedar.

Outside the sliding paper door, the heavy silhouette of Teddy Chin stood guard in the quiet corridor.

Ni Ni lifted a small clay teapot, pouring two fingers of dark, roasted Tieguanyin into a pair of porcelain cups. The scent of roasted iron, charcoal, and damp orchids filled the narrow space.

“The Cutters are quiet in West Seattle,” Ni Ni said. Her voice was a low, measured purr, carrying the refined Cantonese cadence of high-society business backed by absolute violence. “Bookie’s Ancients are staying on their side of the bridge. And Dwight Baxter’s bootleg lab in the Puyallup Barrens is a pile of cold slag.”

She set the teapot down with a soft, dry click.

“You managed all three without firing an unneeded round or bringing the Star into the sector,” she continued, her eyes fixed on his face. “My courier brought back the credstick basket. You left the Bliss capsule on the street.”

“Counterfeits are bad for business,” Ryo said. His voice carried Tanaka Ryo’s lower register, smooth and silvered, acoustically tailored to calm a listener’s heart rate. “Yours, and mine.”

Ni Ni tilted her head slightly. For three slow heartbeats, she studied his face with the razor-sharp appraisal of a senior enforcer parsing an anomaly. She tracked the absolute economy of his posture, noting his micro-expressions: the deliberate delay in his blink cycle, the precise angle at which his jaw met the ambient light, and the real-time adjustment of his vocal timbre against the acoustic dampening of the cedar walls.

As a master of social engineering who spent her life parsing deception, she recognized the reality across the table: a multi-million nuyen corporate asset possessing the fluid, terrifying malleability to shape flesh, voice, and aura into whatever the room required.

A quiet, dangerous stillness settled over the table.

Ni Ni knew what sat across from a creature like that. In a world where every lieutenant, boss, and fixer was plotting a betrayal, sitting across from a partner who could mold himself into her exact psychological ideal was an intoxicating, terrifying luxury. He could be the sharp, lethal peer who understood her tactical language, or the quiet, submissive sanctuary where she could lay down her burden after a blood-soaked shift in the Triad ranks.

Her eyes narrowed as the razor-sharp paranoia of “The Shark” took hold.

Slowly, deliberately, Ni Ni’s right hand slid three inches across the cedar table. Her long, manicured fingers settled lightly against the mother-of-pearl handle of the Ares Predator.

“Who engineered you, Mr. Tanaka?” Ni Ni asked, her voice dropping an octave into cold, iron authority. “Was it Mitsuhama? Or did Wuxing build you to slide into my backroom and listen through my walls?”

Ryo did not draw a weapon. He did not shift his weight or deploy an aura mask to project a combat threat.

Instead, Ryo deliberately dropped the active, aggressive thresholds of his Charisma.

The heavy, magnetic pull that usually filled the air around Tanaka Ryo receded like a falling tide. The unearthly, corporate gravity that forced people to hold their breath dissolved, leaving behind only the simple, physical reality of two people sitting in a dark room beneath the Tacoma rain. Behind his jawbone, a faint metallic click echoed through his throat as the active spell-cartilage relaxed into a quiet baseline.

Ryo reached out, his fingers long and bare, picked up his porcelain cup, and took a slow sip of the hot, bitter tea.

“No one built me for you, Ms. Xiaolu,” Ryo said quietly. His voice dropped into a low, grounded register, carrying the tired cadence of Jin-Young speaking through the corporate frame. “Mitsuhama spent ten years calculating the micro-geometry of my face so fifty million people in Neo-Tokyo would buy corporate bonds without asking why. They carved my vocal cords so a stadium full of strangers would feel less alone for two hours.”

He set the cup down, letting his hands rest flat on the wood.

“It is a very expensive cage,” Ryo continued, meeting her dark eyes directly. “And carrying a face that belongs to a board of directors is exhausting.”

Ni Ni sat motionless. The amber light of the charcoal burner glinted off the mother-of-pearl grip beneath her fingers.

She stared at the man across from her. There was no performance in his posture, no corporate routine running behind his eyes, no attempt to deploy weaponized charm to extract a higher cut or negotiate a contract. He was simply offering her an un-engineered truth in a room shielded from the grid.

The silence stretched between them, filled only by the quiet hum of the steam kettle and the distant, muffled moan of a foghorn out in Commencement Bay.

Slowly, deliberately, Ni Ni’s fingers uncurled from the pearl handle of the heavy pistol. She pulled her hand back, placing it flat on her lap beneath the cedar edge.

Her shoulders, usually locked in the rigid, defensive posture of a Red Pole lieutenant surrounded by enemies, dropped three inches. The sharp, guarded tension along her jawline broke, leaving behind the quiet, hollow exhaustion of a woman who had spent years fighting corrupt superiors and rival syndicates in the dark.

She reached out with both hands, lifted her porcelain cup, and held it close to her mouth, letting the hot, herbal steam wash over her face.

“The tea is good,” Ni Ni murmured, her voice losing its razor-thin edge. “The cook imports the leaves through a small dock in Ningbo. Un-processed. Un-taxed.”

“It tastes like iron,” Ryo said softly. “And rain.”

Ni Ni took a slow drink, closing her eyes for five heartbeats.

For forty minutes, inside the shielded, cedar-lined quiet of Xingfu Chaguan, no one spoke of David Gao, the Eighty-Eights, corporate wars, or the missing fixers of Seattle. They simply sat in the dim warmth beneath the overpass fog, drinking roasted tea while the rain fell over the harbor.

When Ryo stood up to leave, he laid two certified credsticks on the corner of the table to cover the tea, the room, and the quiet night.

Ni Ni remained still as he slid the door open, her posture relaxed, her hands resting flat against the wood away from the heavy pistol.

Ryo stepped out past Teddy Chin into the cold, salt-laden fog of Tacoma, pulling his collar up against the drizzle as the heavy door clicked shut behind him.

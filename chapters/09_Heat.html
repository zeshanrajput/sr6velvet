Heat

The oily smoke of the incinerator barrel thinned into the North Sea drizzle, leaving only a crust of melted ballistic weave and charred resin at the bottom of the rusted drum. Across the canal, the yellow floodlights of the Europort terminal sliced through the fog, silhouetting the massive gantry cranes of Ruhrmetall Logistics as they stacked automated freight containers with rhythmic, hydraulic thuds. Velvet watched the last scrap of blood-spattered synthetic cotton burn to gray ash before stepping back into the shelter of the quay wall.

With a subvocal tap against the throat mic, Velvet cued Ai-chan. The commlink app, an enterprise Personal Assistant package hosted on the Erika Elite, instantly engaged its client-side graphics pipeline. It was no sentient digital mind, merely a ruthlessly efficient model parsing network telemetry, monitoring local sensor feeds, and orchestrating tools across Velvet’s personal area network. Ai-chan queried the ambient lighting parameters, calculated the refractive index of the falling rain, and projected a tailored navy cashmere trench over Velvet’s shoulders. To any dockworker or harbor drone scanning the wharf, the illusion was immaculate, complete with crisp lapels, realistic wool physics, and matte silver buttons catching the sodium glare. Beneath the holographic light, however, the damp Dutch gale bit straight into the bare SecureTech skinshield, slick with sulfur residue.

Even with a fire ancestor coaxed into the hearth of the ribs, the North Sea chill worked into the marrow. The ancestral spirit rested warm against the sternum, radiating dry, talismanic heat that kept the blood moving, but it could not stop sixty kilometers of maritime damp from clinging to exposed skin.

Velvet pushed through the frosted glass door of De Vries & Zoon, an outfitter tucked between two freight forwarders along the canal quay. Radiator heat carried the alkaline bite of dry tailoring chalk and hot lanolin from the bolts overhead, heavy enough to push back the bitter chicory simmering in an enamel pot behind the counter. Rolls of double-faced Antwerp wool and oiled sea-canvas were stacked to the acoustic tile ceiling, deadening the low-frequency thrum of container tugs out in the basin.

Behind the basting table, an elderly tailor in a corduroy vest looked up, a yellowed wooden measuring tape resting across his stooped shoulders. The man wiped his steel shears with an oily rag, his pale eyes cataloging Velvet’s posture with the indifferent precision of an old port tradesman.

“Closed in ten,” the tailor said in Flemish-accented English, laying the shears on a cutting block. “Unless you take off the rack.”

“Off the rack is fine,” Velvet said. The syllables came out in Lawrence Duval’s precise, continental French cadence, though the sudden shift forced a dull ache through the base of the skull as the larynx cartilage settled into the new vocal register. “Charcoal overcoat. Heavy knit underneath. Something that moves quietly through customs offices.”

The tailor stepped from behind the laminate counter and reached out to gauge the shoulder seam of Duval’s navy trench. His dry, calloused fingers passed straight through the holographic projection, striking the cold, synthetic polymer of the skinshield beneath.

The old man pulled his hand back smoothly, squinting through the blue wireframe of the AR garment at the bare torso and the faint red telemetry markers Ai-chan was flashing along the dermal seams.

“The light show is very modern, meneer,” the tailor murmured, his tone as dry as pumice. “Very sharp for a video meet in Brussels, but light does not stop fifty kilometers of Maas wind.”

“No,” Duval said softly. A silent subvocal impulse to Ai-chan dissolved the navy blazer into the yellow bulb-light of the shop, leaving only the neutral, charcoal-grey line of the skinshield against the damp pine floorboards. “It does not.”

The tailor measured the scorch marks along the dermal sleeve with a single indifferent flick of his thumb before turning to the wall rack. In a port city where cargo went missing between Ghent and Europort every night, an unclad customer with certified creds was merely another Tuesday transaction. He pulled a heavy bolt of double-faced Belgian wool from the rack, unrolling three yards across the basting board with a satisfying, muffled thud.

“Stand on the round,” the old man grunted, nodding toward a low oak pedestal before a three-panel dressing mirror.

Velvet stepped onto the wood. As the cold brass tips of the measuring tape brushed collarbone and spine, Velvet shifted his features subtly. Beneath the living skin, the biological levers responded to conscious will: the jawline squared a fraction of a millimeter to support Duval’s crisp Gallic vowels, the clavicle broadened to hold the heavier drape of European tailoring, and the ribcage compressed slightly to steady the lungs. The internal bone-latch clicked deep behind the ear, a brief spike of metabolic heat swallowed without a flinch.

In the three-panel mirror, the lingering ghost of the previous forty-eight hours seemed to flicker against the glass. In Ghent, two automated freight trucks loaded with mass-produced, smiling green dragon plushies had been bought with a Saeder-Krupp Johnson’s caved-in skull and the deafening clatter of heavy machine gun fire. Yet forty minutes before the shooting tore the loading dock apart, beside a muddy canal pitch, Velvet had watched hardened syndics drop their weapons to shout for their children in the sudden downpour. The memory settled deep behind Duval’s newly sculpted eyes, anchoring the quiet, stubborn endurance of ordinary people surviving in the shadow of corporate titans.

The tailor tossed a charcoal overcoat over Duval’s shoulders. The weight hit with immediate, anchoring force, ten pounds of dense, boiled wool pressing the fire ancestor’s warmth back into the chest. The scratch of the raw fiber against the neck was abrasive, grounding, and completely real.

The old man pinned the left cuff with three swift jabs of his needle. “Good wool has memory,” he muttered, smoothing the back seam with the flat of his palm. “You treat it right, it holds the shape of the man wearing it long after the weather breaks.”

“Let us hope so,” Duval said, sliding his arms through the heavy silk lining and stepping down from the pedestal. “Monsieur, if I may ask. Word around the quay is that the dock families were stirred up after what happened in Ghent. I had hoped to make their acquaintance before my transport departs.”

The tailor paused, smoothing the chalk line along the lapel before looking up. “A messy business near the rail yards. S-K security drones have been buzzing the canal since dawn. Best not to ask questions near the cranes. Best not to be seen.”

“I agree completely,” Duval replied with a faint, polite inclination of the head. “And yet, a man in my line of trade cannot leave the Maas without speaking to the people who control the water.”

The old man tapped the end of his wooden tape against his palm, weighing the cut of the wool against the French accent. “Walk four blocks past the lock gate. There is a bathhouse behind the eel smoker with a red tile roof and no Matrix sign. The dock stewards go there to sweat out the cold and wash the diesel from their hair. Tell the girl at the counter that Pieter sent you for the birch steam. If anyone is willing to talk business while S-K sweeps the harbor, you will find them there.”

Velvet pulled three certified credsticks from the commlink pouch, laid them on the basting table with extra for discretion, and buttoned the heavy wool coat to the throat. Stepping out into the sulfuric Europort fog, the North Sea wind slammed against the heavy Belgian wool and broke harmlessly against it, leaving the vessel inside dry, warm, and entirely opaque.

3. The Dark Wings

Under the hundred-thousand-watt daylight arrays of the Wuxing soundstage, there was no shadow for a soul to hide in. Ryo was seventeen, his skin artificially saturated with the radiant, cross-border vitality mandated by the label’s branding executives. The three-acre floor of Astral Athletics: Idol Showdown was a labyrinth of chrome obstacles and synchronized cameras, every square inch precisely calculated to extract maximum fan resonance.

Then, the secondary transformer beneath the floor emitted a high-frequency scream, and the world went black.

In the sudden, heavy dark of the soundstage, the security feeds died. Ryo slipped through a heavy fire door, navigating the backstage corridors by the low-light adjustments of his eyes. He ended up in the VIP green room, a room of synthetic leather and Wuxing-patented air scrubbers.

Luna was already there, sitting on a low couch in the corner.

The green room was silent, but it was not private. Even in the dark, the watcher spirit bound to his aura hung in the astral plane—a cold, parasitic eye that watched the temperature of his blood and the baseline alignment of his spiritual energy.

“You’re late,” Luna said.

Her voice did not carry the artificial, breathy pitch of her stage persona. In the dark, there was a minute, high-frequency hum from the actuators in her cervical vertebrae as she turned her head. Her eyes—perfect, glassy Wuxing-patented optics—did not blink. They shuttered with a faint, mechanical click as she adjusted the exposure to the dim light of the green room, the internal autofocus clicking like a beetle.

“The corridor was blocked,” Ryo said. The syllables slipped out in the soft, breathy cadence of Ji-yoo, a habitual pitch that made him wince in the dark. He caught himself, forcing his larynx down, but the correction was too sharp, landing in the rigid, low-toned register of his masculine form. “I… I mean. It is clear now.”

He sat on the arm of the chair opposite her. He kept his hands flat on his knees. In his head, the scripts fought for control: the deferential tilt of the chin mandated for Ji-yoo, the balanced, symmetrical shoulder weight of Ryo. In the absolute dark, away from the immediate gaze of the watcher spirit that lingered in the astral, he tried to find the flat, raw vowels of the Manila streets—his own, un-sculpted voice. The silence inside his throat felt like empty space where a tongue had been cut out. He could only stutter between the registers, a mechanical skipping of the needle.

Luna reached up, her fingers tracing the edge of her collarbone where the Wuxing dermal plating met her skin. In the dim light, the chrome seams of her shoulder joint gleamed—thin, silver lines that split the porcelain perfection of her patented skin blueprint.

“They adjusted my biometrics this morning,” she said, her voice flat, devoid of the melody Wuxing sold. “Lung capacity is up twelve percent for the choreography. My chest feels hollow. Like a tin can.”

“They plane the cartilage first,” he said. He was speaking in Ryo’s voice now, but the cadence was too soft, the feminine elegance of Ji-yoo’s phrasing bleeding into the sentence structure. “Then they weave… she… they weave the spells into the bone to keep the lungs from collapsing. It feels dry behind the ribs for the first year.” He felt the linguistic exhaustion settle in—a heavy, mental vertigo. He was losing track of the pronouns in his own thoughts, the boundary lines of his identity blurring into a soup of corporate directives.

“Does it stop?”

“No,” he whispered, struggling to hold Ryo’s masculine baseline stable. “You just learn where the pain is and avoid looking at it.”

In the dark, she reached out, her fingers touching the back of his hand. Her skin was warm, but her fingers were slightly stiff—the corporate cybernetics in her joints lacked the subtle, irregular micro-tremors of native flesh. Through the physical contact, Ryo felt the cold, quiet void in her aura. She was down to a fraction of a human baseline, her soul shaved away to accommodate the high-performance reflex systems and optic arrays.

“You… you’re both, aren’t you?” she whispered.

The watcher spirit bound to his aura drifted closer in the astral, its presence a cold, oily smear against his skin, testing for any deviation in his emotional matrix. Ryo did not pull his hand away. He held his mind perfectly still, locking his emotions behind a wall of clinical, sterile diagnostics.

“They’re bringing the grid back online,” Ryo said.

The transformer beneath the floorboards began to whine, a high, thin sound that vibrated through the synthetic leather of the couch. The emergency lights in the green room flickered, casting a dim red glow over the chrome seams on her neck.

“Hana,” she whispered into the dark. “My real name is Hana.”

Ryo tried to calculate the legal penalties attached to the syllables, but the numbers melted away, leaving only the cool, un-sculpted weight of her name on his tongue. “Jin,” he murmured, his voice cracking between his assigned registers. “I think it was Jin.”

She pulled her fingers back a split-second before the main grid caught. The soundstage flooded with blinding, hundred-thousand-watt white light, instantly bleaching the red emergency glow from the chrome seams of her neck. When the director entered, the warm, immaculate pop star was already sitting perfectly straight on the synthetic leather couch, her eyes wide and soft.

Ryo stood up, his face effortlessly sliding back into polite, calibrated radiance.
